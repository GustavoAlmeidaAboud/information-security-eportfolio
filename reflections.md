# Part 4: Critical Reflection (Rolfe et al., 2001)

This reflection follows Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper's (2001) "What? So what? Now what?" model. I chose it because it moves from describing an experience to analysing it and then to concrete changes in practice, which suits a module that is itself about turning analysis into action.

## 1. What? (Description of Experience)

I took the Security and Risk Management module while working full time as a Modern Workplace Engineer specialising in cybersecurity. My day-to-day work centres on endpoint management, identity and the security configuration of cloud productivity platforms. I therefore started the module with practical, control-focused experience but less exposure to formal, structured methods of assessing and communicating risk.

Across the twelve units the module covered the risk management process, qualitative and quantitative approaches to risk assessment, threat modelling methods such as STRIDE, DREAD and attack trees, the move towards hybrid models, security standards such as PCI-DSS, and business continuity and disaster recovery. It also included collaborative discussions and a team project with a status document in Unit 6 and a final deliverable in Unit 11.

Overall, the module went well for me in terms of learning, but it was challenging to commit to it consistently. Balancing a demanding job with study meant that I often worked through the material in concentrated blocks rather than steadily each week. As a result, my participation in the collaborative discussion forums and team activities was more limited than I had intended, and that is the central experience I reflect on here.

## 2. So What? (Analysis and Interpretation)

**The Security and Risk Management process.** The most significant change in my thinking was moving from seeing security as a set of controls to seeing it as a risk management process. In my role it is natural to think in terms of configurations: enforcing multi-factor authentication, hardening devices or applying conditional access policies. The module showed me that these controls only make sense when they are linked to identified assets, threats, likelihood and business impact. Frameworks such as NIST SP 800-30 (NIST, 2012) and ISO/IEC 27005 (ISO, 2022) frame risk assessment as a repeatable cycle of identification, analysis, evaluation and treatment, and that cycle gave me a vocabulary for explaining why a control matters, not just how to deploy it.

Threat modelling was the area I found most directly transferable. STRIDE offers a systematic way to ask what could go wrong with a system (Shostack, 2014), and attack trees break an attacker's goal into concrete paths (Schneier, 1999). I could see how these methods would make my own work more rigorous, for example by modelling how an attacker might move from a compromised user account to sensitive data in a collaboration platform, rather than relying on experience and intuition alone.

The comparison between qualitative and quantitative risk assessment also challenged me. In practice, risk is often expressed as "high, medium, low" heat maps. Hubbard and Seiersen (2016) argue that such scales can hide uncertainty and give a false sense of precision, and that even simple quantitative estimates support better decisions. I do not think qualitative methods are worthless, as they are fast and easy to communicate to non-specialists, but I now see the value of hybrid approaches that make assumptions explicit and allow decisions to be defended to business stakeholders.

**My individual contribution and experience as a team member.** Being honest about my contribution is uncomfortable but necessary. Because of work pressures, I engaged with the team activities and discussion forums less than I should have. When I fell behind, I felt frustrated and somewhat guilty, and this created a cycle: the further behind I was, the harder it felt to rejoin conversations that had already moved on. Looking back, I tended to treat study as something to "catch up on" alone rather than as a shared activity, which is the opposite of what collaborative learning requires.

This matters professionally as well as academically. Security and risk management is rarely an individual task; risk assessments depend on input from system owners, users and leadership, and a good analysis that is not shared or discussed has limited value. The difficulty I had in staying engaged with the team mirrors a real workplace risk, in which security specialists become isolated and their analysis is not integrated into decisions. Research on part-time and working students shows that balancing work, study and personal life is a common and significant source of stress (Lowe and Gayle, 2007), which helps me see my experience as a predictable challenge to plan for rather than a personal failing to ignore.

**Impact on my professional and personal development.** Despite these difficulties, the module has had a clear effect on how I approach my work. I am more inclined to start from assets and threats before choosing controls, to document assumptions and to think about business continuity and recovery, not only prevention. Personally, the module made me confront how I manage time and commitments, and showed me that resilience is not about working harder in short bursts but about building sustainable routines.

## 3. Now What? (Actionable Outcomes and Future Development)

In my professional practice, I will apply structured threat modelling, using STRIDE and attack trees, when designing or changing workplace and identity configurations, and I will record the results in a simple risk register so that decisions are traceable. I will also try to support qualitative ratings with rough quantitative estimates when presenting risks to stakeholders.

In my studies, I will plan my workload at the start of each module by blocking fixed weekly time for study and, in particular, for collaborative activities, so that forum posts and team tasks are not the first things to slip when work becomes busy. If I fall behind, I will contact my team and tutor early rather than withdrawing, since early communication is itself a form of risk management.

For my wider development, I will maintain this e-portfolio as an ongoing record of learning and reflection, and I will set a professional development goal of formalising my risk management knowledge further, for example through recognised security risk frameworks and certifications relevant to my role.
